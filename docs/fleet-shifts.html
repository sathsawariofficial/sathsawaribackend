Fleet Shifts

A group is a fleet of vehicles and drivers. A shift is one vehicle taking one driver and a set of passengers along an ordered route, on one day, in one direction.

01 / the modelWhat the system is made of

Drivers and their vehicles already existed for carpooling. Everything to the right of them is new.

A driver creates a group and becomes its owner, which in this product is the same thing as its manager. Other drivers ask to join, bringing vehicles, offering only to drive, or only lending a vehicle. Passengers ask to join too. Nothing is granted on request — the manager decides.

Once the fleet exists, the manager builds shifts out of it. Each shift pins one vehicle and one driver to a window of time and hangs an ordered list of stops off it, with every seat of the vehicle accounted for.

02 / anatomyWhat a shift actually is

One vehicle, one driver, one day, one direction — and a route the manager arranges by hand.

The stops are sent in the order they should be driven, and each seat hangs off the stop its passenger waits at. Every physical seat of the vehicle gets a row, so an empty seat is just as visible as a taken one and the manager can see the gaps left to fill.

A seat carries the gender it is kept for. The seat's gender and the person sitting in it must always agree, so nobody ever lands in a seat reserved for the other gender. The manager can re-declare that gender while swapping the occupant, which is how a male rider is exchanged for a female one in a single request.

03 / directionsGoing and coming back are two shifts

The passenger experiences one journey. The system holds two entirely separate objects.

A pickup and a drop-off are never linked. They have their own routes, their own stop order, their own seat assignments, and they are cancelled independently. That is deliberate: the evening rarely retraces the morning, and somebody who rides in one direction often does not ride in the other.

04 / authorityWho may do what

Nothing in the code tests a role by name. Every check asks the database what the caller's role is allowed to do.

That is what makes the system extensible: an admin can invent a new role and map permissions onto it, and the group and shift endpoints honour it immediately without a deploy. A sub-manager exists to take the shift-building load off the manager, and holds none of the membership permissions.

The rule you insisted on

A sub-manager can only be promoted from somebody who is already an approved driver-member of that same fleet. Anyone else comes back in the skipped list with a reason, never promoted. The owner can never be demoted — that would leave the fleet with nobody in charge.

A role's default permissions are written only the first time that role is created. If an admin later strips shift.cancel from sub-managers, a restart will not quietly hand it back.

05 / safetyWhat the system refuses to do

Every rule is checked before a single row is written, and the whole shift is written in one transaction.

The clash rules are the ones with teeth. A vehicle and a driver can each be in one place at a time, and the check spans both fleet shifts and the existing carpool rides — because a driver is the same person in both halves of the product.

Everything else that is refused

  • A seat whose declared gender does not match the passenger being put in it.
  • The same passenger holding two seats on one shift — judged against the shift as it will look after the change, not just the seats in the request.
  • A seat number the vehicle does not have.
  • A driver, vehicle or passenger who is not an approved member of that fleet.
  • A vehicle joining a fleet without declaring how many seats it has.
  • A group created with no vehicle at all.
  • Cancelling a shift that starts within two hours — the same guard carpool rides already use.
  • Deleting a fleet that still has shifts ahead of it.
  • Deleting a role that is still held by a member, or renaming a system role.
  • A driver leaving a fleet while shifts still expect them behind the wheel.
  • A driver leaving while a vehicle they lent is carrying a shift — even one somebody else drives, because their vehicles leave with them.
  • The owner leaving their own fleet — they delete it instead.
  • A passenger being seated on two shifts that overlap in time.
  • A shift scheduled in the past, or rescheduled backwards into it.
  • Rescheduling a shift that departs within two hours — people are already on their way to the stop.
  • Lowering a vehicle's seat count beneath a shift already built on it.

06 / test flowThe order to call things in

The Postman collection is arranged in exactly this order, and chains the ids for you through test scripts.

These steps really are sequential — each one depends on state the previous one created, which is why they are numbered.

  1. Register and activate the owner driver

    Register, verify the OTP with ACTIVATE_DRIVER, log in, set a pin. The fleet's owner is an ordinary driver.

    POST /driver/register → POST /otp/verify → POST /driver/login → POST /driver/pin

  2. Register a vehicle with its seats

    This is the step that did not exist before. A vehicle must declare numberOfSeats before it can ever join a fleet; AC and heating ride along with it.

    POST /vehicle/register → POST /otp/verify (ACTIVATE_VEHICLE)

  3. Do the same for a second driver and two passengers

    Use one passenger of each gender — the seat rules only become visible with both. Passengers activate with ACTIVATE_PASSENGER, their own operation.

    POST /passenger/register → POST /otp/verify → POST /passenger/login

  4. Have each passenger file their weekly travel form

    One call carries the whole week, one row per day per direction. Switch off Tuesday's drop leg to see a passenger who rides in only in the morning.

    PUT /passenger/schedule

  5. Create the group

    The owner's vehicle goes in at creation. The owner's membership row is written at the same time, carrying the group_owner role.

    POST /group

  6. Everyone else finds the fleet, then asks to join

    Search returns the joinable fleets with your own request status on each row — this is the only way to learn a group id. Then the second driver requests with joinType: both and their vehicle, and both passengers request on their own tokens.

    GET /group/search → POST /group/request/driver · POST /passenger/group/request

  7. Approve the queue in one call

    List the pending requests to get the membership row ids, then approve drivers, vehicles and passengers together. The response separates what applied from what was skipped and why.

    GET /group/requests → PATCH /group/requests

  8. Promote the second driver to sub-manager

    Only works because they are already inside the fleet. Try it with a driver who never joined and watch it land in skipped.

    PATCH /group/submanagers

  9. Read the travel forms, then build the morning shift

    Pull the forms for Monday pickup, then send the stops in route order with the passengers seated under them. Set makeTemplate to save the shape.

    GET /group/passengers/schedules → POST /shift

  10. Build the evening drop shift separately

    Different route, reversed stop order, different drop points. Nothing links it to the morning.

    POST /shift

  11. Prove the clash rule

    Try a second shift on the same vehicle overlapping the morning window. It should fail with “Vehicle already has a ride or a shift scheduled for this duration”.

    POST /shift — expected to fail

  12. Swap the riders and reshuffle seats

    Move the female passenger into the male's seat and vice versa in one request, re-declaring each seat's gender as you go. Then free a seat by sending an empty passengerId.

    PUT /shift/seats

  13. Look at it from every side

    The manager's roster, the driver's own shifts, and the passenger's own shifts — the last two are the same handler seen through different tokens.

    GET /shift · GET /shift/mine · GET /passenger/shifts · GET /shift/detail

  14. Push the departure back without losing the seats

    Move the window and the individual stop times in one call. Stops are matched by sequence, so the seats stay attached and the clash check re-runs ignoring this shift's own row.

    PATCH /shift

  15. Have somebody leave

    A passenger leaving has their upcoming seats freed automatically. A driver is refused while shifts still expect them at the wheel.

    DELETE /passenger/group/leave · DELETE /group/leave

  16. Reuse the template, then clean up

    Fetch the template, prefill the create form with it, change the date and post it back to the same create endpoint. Then cancel the shifts and delete the fleet.

    GET /shift/templates → POST /shift → DELETE /shift → DELETE /group

07 / oversightWhat the admin can see

The fleet feature created three whole populations the admin console could not see: passenger accounts, groups, and shifts.

The admin already had drivers, vehicles, rides and broadcasts. Everything this feature added was invisible to it — a passenger could be suspended nowhere, a fleet could misbehave with no way to shut it down, and a shift could go wrong with no way to look at who was on it.

Moderation is deliberately two-tiered. Switching an account or a fleet off stops it being used while leaving its history intact; deleting is separate, permanent, and unpicks every reference to it.

08 / recordsNothing that mattered is thrown away

Deleting a row copies it to its own archive table first, the way this codebase already treated drivers, vehicles and rides.

The fleet feature added things worth keeping, and two of them were being dropped on the floor: a passenger's standing travel form, and a role an admin deleted. Both are now archived — the role together with the permission codes it held at that moment, written inline so the record still reads correctly long after those permissions have changed.

Shifts and memberships are never hard-deleted at all. A cancelled shift, a rejected request, a member who left: each is a status on a row that stays exactly where it was.

Driven end to end against a live database. One hundred and nine calls covering every endpoint — the fleet feature, the admin console and the existing carpool — all behaved as expected, including the refusals that prove each rule. Every saved response in the Postman collection is a real one captured from that run, not written by hand.

Two faults only the run could find. The app could not restart: seeding a permission passed a struct already carrying a fresh id, GORM folded that id into the lookup, so every boot after the first tried to insert a code that already existed and panicked. And no passenger could use their session: the token verifier knew driver, admin and open tokens and fell through to “invalid token type” for the passenger one, so every passenger route rejected a perfectly good login. Both fixed.

One pre-existing typo left untouched, because changing it would alter responses your app may already match on: Deleted_Successfully and DELETE_Failed both read “updated”, so every delete endpoint — old and new — replies “updated successfully”.
